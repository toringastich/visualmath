A 2×2 matrix is just four numbers. But it is also a linear transformation:
each column is a vector, and together they warp the gridlines of 2-D space.
The determinant is a fifth number, built from those four — and it carries a
startling amount of meaning. It tells you whether the transformation stretches
space, flips it, flattens it, or can be undone at all. Four numbers in, one
number out, and somehow it knows everything. How?

My name is Torin, and I just graduated from high school. In my senior calculus
class we used determinants mostly for symbol-pushing — a formula, a means to an
end. Then 3Blue1Brown's videos showed me that a determinant is far more than a
formula, and I wanted to bring that kind of visual, hands-on intuition into a
sandbox you can actually play in, the way you play in Desmos. That's why I
built Warp, and why I wrote this lesson to teach with it.

By the end you won't have memorized a single formula, but you'll have a strong,
almost physical sense of what a determinant is. You'll get there the same way I
taught myself linear algebra: by watching it move, and by changing things
yourself. Every diagram below is a live copy of Warp — so don't just read it.
Grab it, type in it, break it.

## The one number is an area

Start in two dimensions. A matrix takes the humble unit square — one unit wide,
one unit tall, area exactly 1 — and warps it into a parallelogram. In the panel
below, the matrix `M` is doing exactly that: the shaded patch is where the unit
square lands, and the row labelled `det(M)` reports a single number for it. To
start, that number is 6.

<WarpEmbed state={DET_AREA} />

That number is the punchline of the whole lesson: **the determinant is the
factor by which the transformation scales area.** The unit square began with
area 1, so the determinant is simply the area of the parallelogram it becomes.

See it for yourself. Click any entry of `M` and type a new value — the
parallelogram stretches or shears, and `det(M)` moves right along with its
area. Try making `M` have a smaller determinant, then try making a larger one!
Keep experimenting right here, or open the scene in a full window if you'd like
more room.

## Drive it to zero, and straight past it

If the determinant is an area, what would it mean for it to be *zero*? An area
of zero is a shape with no interior — something crushed perfectly flat.

The matrix in the next panel starts with a determinant of −1. Press play and
watch the transformation unfold gradually; replay it as many times as you like,
or grab the slider on the right and scrub to any moment in between. Halfway
through, the whole grid thins to a single line — that's the instant the
determinant passes through zero, where two-dimensional space has been flattened
onto one dimension. Keep going and space reopens on the far side,
mirror-reversed.

<WarpEmbed state={DET_FLIP} />

That flip is the second thing a determinant tells you: **orientation.** A
positive determinant keeps space oriented the way it started; a negative one
turns it inside out, the way a mirror swaps left and right; zero is the
knife-edge between them, the moment space is squeezed so hard it loses a
dimension. So the determinant's size tells you *how much* area changed, and its
sign tells you *whether space got flipped* along the way. Try retyping the
matrix and replaying — some transformations flip, some don't.

## In three dimensions, it's a volume

None of that was special to the flat plane. Step up to three dimensions and the
unit square becomes the unit *cube*, and the determinant becomes the factor by
which the transformation scales **volume**.

Drag inside the panel to orbit the scene. The cube of volume 1 has landed as a
slanted box (called a parallelepiped) and its volume is exactly what `det(M)`
reports (it begins at 2). A determinant of zero here would mean the box has been
crushed flat into a plane, a line, or a single point: volume gone.

Now make it yours. Change the numbers in the matrix, press play, and watch the
box — and its volume, the determinant — respond. It's the same story as before,
with one more dimension of room to move in.

## Why det(A·B) = det(A) · det(B)

Here's a fact that looks like algebra but is really geometry. Do one
transformation, then another. The first scales area by its determinant; the
second scales *that* by its own determinant. Scalings simply multiply — so the
determinant of the combined transformation has to be the product of the two.
Mathematicians can grind that out with a page of algebra, or you can just watch
it happen.

<WarpEmbed state={DET_COMPOSE} height={520} />

The panel above has three rows: `M`, `N`, and their product `C = M·N`. **`C`
shows both matrices at once** — the single transformation you get by doing `N`
first and then `M`. Its play button walks through that combination one step at a
time: first `N` warps space, scaling area by its determinant, then `M` lands on
top, scaling by its. Watch the running area grow by `N`'s factor, then by
`M`'s — and land exactly on the determinant of `C`. To start, `det(M)` is 2 and
`det(N)` is 3, and sure enough `det(C)` is 6.

To pull the two apart, click the small circle to the left of `M`, or of `N`.
**Toggling one of them on makes just that single matrix warp space**, so you can
read its determinant on its own — then switch back to `C` to see them combined
again. However you slice it, the determinant of the product is the two
determinants multiplied. That's the whole proof, and you can watch it hold for
any numbers you type. Feeling ambitious? Press the plus button to add a third
matrix and fold it into the product too.

## The payoff: when can you undo a matrix?

Now the question the whole lesson has been circling: when can a transformation
be undone? A transformation is *invertible* when every landing spot came from
exactly one starting point — so you can always retrace your steps.

<WarpEmbed state={DET_INVERT} />

Look at what a zero determinant does to that. In the panel, `det(M)` is 0 and
the grid has collapsed onto a single line. Every point on that line is now a
pile-up of a whole line's worth of starting points, stacked on top of one
another. Which one did you come from? There's no way to tell — the information
is gone. The row `inv(M)` says it outright: not invertible.

That's the punchline the subject rests on: **a matrix is invertible exactly
when its determinant is not zero.** Not a rule to memorize — something you just
watched happen. A zero determinant flattens space, flattening destroys
information, and destroyed information can't be recovered. One number — an area,
a volume — told you whether the transformation could be undone before you ever
tried. Nudge an entry so the determinant leaves zero, and watch `inv(M)` spring
back to life.

Everything above ran on Warp — and here's a blank one, entirely yours. Add a

here? Press **Tutorial** for a quick tour. A few challenges worth trying:

- Find a matrix with determinant 1 that *isn't* the identity — a shear. It
  moves every vector, yet changes no area at all.
- Push a determinant below zero and play the animation to feel the flip.
- Switch to 3D, type a `3×3` matrix, and hunt for the determinant hitting
  zero — the moment the box goes flat.
