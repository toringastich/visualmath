The cross product is usually taught as a formula with a mnemonic — a
determinant of a strange matrix with î, ĵ, k̂ jammed into the top row. The
formula is true. It is also the least illuminating possible answer to the
question the cross product actually addresses, which is about a *shape*.

## Two vectors span a parallelogram

Here are v = (2, 1, 0) and w = (−1, 2, 0), both lying flat in the xy-plane.
Together they span a parallelogram — the shaded patch between them. Drag to
orbit the scene and look at it from every side.

<WarpEmbed
  state={CROSS_FLAT}
  caption="v and w span a parallelogram, flat in the xy-plane. Drag to orbit."
  height={480}
/>

Every question the cross product answers starts with this patch: **how big is
it, and which way does it face?**

## One vector answers both

The row u = cross(v, w) computes (0, 0, 5): a vector pointing straight up,
perpendicular to both v and w — perpendicular, in fact, to the whole patch.

<WarpEmbed
  state={CROSS_UP}
  caption="u = cross(v, w) = (0, 0, 5), perpendicular to the patch — and norm(u) = 5 is exactly its area."
  height={480}
/>

Its length is no accident. norm(u) = 5 is *exactly the parallelogram's area*.
And its direction follows the right-hand rule: curl your fingers from v
toward w, and your thumb points along u. One vector, both answers — size as
length, orientation as direction.

## It follows the parallelogram

Tilt w out of the plane — w = (−1, 2, 1) — and the parallelogram tilts with
it. The cross product follows: u = (1, −2, 5) leans exactly enough to stay
perpendicular. The row dot(v, u) = 0 is the receipt. Perpendicularity isn't a
coincidence of the first example; it's built into the definition.

<WarpEmbed
  state={CROSS_TILT}
  caption="Tilt w out of the plane and u leans to stay perpendicular — dot(v, u) = 0, always. Edit w's entries and watch."
  height={480}
/>

## Now it's yours

Two experiments to close the loop:

- Make v and w **parallel** and the parallelogram collapses to a line — the
  cross product returns the zero vector. No patch, no normal.
- Make them **perpendicular unit vectors** and the area is exactly 1 — the
  cross product of two axes is the third axis.

The cross product is the parallelogram, wearing a vector costume.
