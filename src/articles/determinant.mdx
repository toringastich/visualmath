A 2×2 matrix is just four numbers. But it is also a linear transformation,
where each column represents a vector that warps the gridlines of 2-D space.
The determinant is just a fifth number, calculated using the four numbers in a
2×2 matrix. This one number also has a very important visual component, and it
tells you whether the linear transformation encoded by the matrix stretches
space, flips it, flattens it, or can be undone at all. Four numbers in, one
number out, and it knows everything. How?

My name is Torin, and I just graduated from high school. In my senior year I
took a calculus class where we used determinants mostly for symbol-pushing, as
a means to an end. 3Blue1Brown's videos taught me that determinants are much
more than a formula, and I wanted to bring those visuals to a sandbox
environment like Desmos where you can actually "play around" with visual math
rather than just watching. That's why I built Warp, and this lesson to show how
to learn using it.

By the end of this lesson you will not have memorized any formulas, but you
will have a very strong sense of what a determinant is. You'll get there mainly
by seeing and experimenting, which is exactly how I self-taught linear algebra.

## The one number is an area

Start in two dimensions. A matrix takes the humble unit square — one unit wide,
one unit tall, area exactly 1 — and warps it into a parallelogram. Here is
`M`, and the shaded patch is where the unit square lands. The row `det(M)`
reads **6**.

<WarpEmbed
  state={DET_AREA}
  caption="M = [[3, 1], [0, 2]] warps the unit square into a parallelogram of area 6 — and det(M) = 6."
/>

**The determinant is the factor by which the transformation scales area.** The
unit square had area 1; its image has area 6; so `det(M) = 6`. Now, change an
entry of `M` and watch it warp space — you'll notice the parallelogram's area
and the determinant are always the same. You're welcome to keep experimenting
here, or you can open the link if you'd like more space.

## Drive it to zero, and straight past it

If the determinant is an area, what would it mean for it to be *zero*? An area
of zero is a shape with no interior — a shape that has been crushed flat.

Here `M` has `det(M) = −1`. Watch the transformation happen gradually. You can
hit the play button again, or pick up the slider from the right and scrub to
any point. Halfway through, the entire grid thins to a single line: at that
instant the determinant passes through zero, and all of two-dimensional space
has been flattened onto one dimension. Then space *reopens* on the other side —
mirror-reversed.

<WarpEmbed
  state={DET_FLIP}
  caption="det(M) = −1. Press ▶: at the midpoint the grid collapses to a line, then reopens flipped."
/>

So the sign of the determinant carries a second piece of information the area
alone can't: **orientation.** A positive determinant keeps space oriented the
way it started; a negative determinant turns it inside out, the way a
reflection swaps left and right. A determinant of zero is the instant between
them, the moment space is squashed so hard it loses a dimension. The
determinant's magnitude tells you *how much* area changed; its sign tells you
*whether space got flipped* on the way.

## In three dimensions, it's a volume

Nothing about that story was special to the plane. Move to 3D and the unit
square becomes the unit *cube*, and the determinant becomes the factor by
which the transformation scales **volume**.

<WarpEmbed
  state={DET_VOLUME}
  caption="A 3×3 matrix warps the unit cube into this parallelepiped. det(M) = 2 — its volume. Drag to orbit."
/>

Same idea, one dimension up: the cube of volume 1 lands as a slanted box — a
parallelepiped — of volume 2, so `det(M) = 2`. Drag your cursor to orbit
around it. A determinant of zero here would mean the cube gets crushed flat
into a plane, a line, or a point — volume gone. The determinant is the single
number that measures "how much space" survives the transformation, in any
dimension.

Now you try. Just change the numbers in the matrix and hit the play button to
see the animation. Watch how the slanted box and its volume, the determinant,
are affected.

## Why det(AB) = det(A) · det(B)

Here is a fact that looks like it belongs to algebra, and turns out to belong
to geometry. Do one transformation, then another. The first scales area (or
volume) by its determinant; the second scales *that* by its own determinant.
Scalings multiply. So the determinant of the combined transformation must be
the product of the two determinants. Mathematicians can prove that with lots
and lots of calculations, or you can see it proven visually right here on Warp.

<WarpEmbed
  state={DET_COMPOSE}
  caption="C = M·N. det(M) = 2, det(N) = 3, det(C) = 6. Press the circle next to N, and watch. Do the same for M. You'll see area scale ×3, then ×2."
  height={520}
/>

Watch it happen: `N` scales area by 3, then `M` scales that by 2, and the
composite `C = M·N` scales area by 6. The identity `det(AB) = det(A)·det(B)` —
which is a small chore to prove by grinding through the entries — is obvious
the instant you read the determinant as an area factor. Two scalings in a row
multiply. That's the whole proof.

If you're still wary, try it with different numbers and watch each animation.
If you're feeling extra ambitious, hit the "plus" button to add another matrix.
Add it into the `C = M·N` equation and watch three matrices in a row.

## The payoff: when can you undo a matrix?

Now the question everything has been building toward. A transformation is
*invertible* when you can undo it — when every landing spot came from exactly
one starting point, so you can always find your way back.

Watch what a determinant of zero does to that. Here `det(M) = 0`, and the grid
has collapsed onto a single line. Every point on that line is the image of a
whole line's worth of starting points — they've been piled on top of each
other. The information about where things came from is *gone*. And the row
`inv(M)` says exactly that:

<WarpEmbed
  state={DET_INVERT}
  caption="det(M) = 0 flattens the plane onto a line — and inv(M) reports 'Not invertible (det = 0)'."
/>

There it is, the punchline the whole subject hinges on: **a matrix is
invertible when its determinant is not zero.** That isn't a rule to memorize,
rather something you just watched happen. Zero determinant means space got
flattened, flattening destroys information, and destroyed information can't be
recovered. One number — an area, a volume — told you whether the transformation
could be undone before you tried.
