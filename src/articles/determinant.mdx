## The one number is an area

Start in two dimensions. A matrix takes the humble unit square — one unit wide,
one unit tall, area exactly 1 — and warps it into a parallelogram. Here is
`M`, and the shaded patch is where the unit square lands. The row `det(M)`
reads **6**.

<WarpEmbed
  state={DET_AREA}
  caption="M = [[3, 1], [0, 2]] warps the unit square into a parallelogram of area 6 — and det(M) = 6."
/>

That is the whole secret, in one sentence: **the determinant is the factor by
which the transformation scales area.** The unit square had area 1; its image
has area 6; so `det(M) = 6`. Change an entry of `M` and watch the two numbers
move together — the parallelogram's area and the determinant are never
different, because they are the same thing.

This already pays off the mysterious formula. For `M = [[a, b], [c, d]]`, the
area of that parallelogram works out to `ad − bc`. The incantation you
memorized was measuring a shape all along.

## Drive it to zero, and straight past it

If the determinant is an area, what would it mean for it to be *zero*? An area
of zero is a shape with no interior — a shape that has been crushed flat.

Here `M` has `det(M) = −1`. Press play and watch the transformation happen
gradually. Halfway through, the entire grid thins to a single line: at that
instant the determinant passes through zero, and all of two-dimensional space
has been flattened onto one dimension. Then space *reopens* on the other side —
mirror-reversed.

<WarpEmbed
  state={DET_FLIP}
  caption="det(M) = −1. Press ▶: at the midpoint the grid collapses to a line (det = 0), then reopens flipped."
/>

So the sign of the determinant carries a second piece of information the area
alone can't: **orientation.** A positive determinant keeps space oriented the
way it started; a negative determinant turns it inside out, the way a
reflection swaps left and right. Zero is the knife-edge between them — the
moment space is squashed so hard it loses a dimension. The determinant's
magnitude tells you *how much* area changed; its sign tells you *whether space
got flipped* on the way.

## In three dimensions, it's a volume

Nothing about that story was special to the plane. Move to 3D and the unit
square becomes the unit *cube*, and the determinant becomes the factor by
which the transformation scales **volume**.

<WarpEmbed
  state={DET_VOLUME}
  caption="A 3×3 matrix warps the unit cube into this parallelepiped. det(M) = 6 — its volume. Drag to orbit."
/>

Same idea, one dimension up: the cube of volume 1 lands as a slanted box — a
parallelepiped — of volume 6, so `det(M) = 6`. Orbit around it. A determinant
of zero here would mean the cube gets crushed flat into a plane, a line, or a
point — volume gone. The determinant is the single number that measures "how
much space" survives the transformation, in any dimension.

## Why det(AB) = det(A) · det(B)

Here is a fact that looks like it belongs to algebra, and turns out to belong
to geometry. Do one transformation, then another. The first scales area (or
volume) by its determinant; the second scales *that* by its own determinant.
Scalings multiply. So the determinant of the combined transformation must be
the product of the two determinants — no computation required, just the
meaning of the word.

<WarpEmbed
  state={DET_COMPOSE}
  caption="C = M·N. det(M) = 2, det(N) = 3, det(C) = 6. Press ▶ to apply N then M — area scales ×3, then ×2."
  height={520}
/>

Watch it happen: `N` scales area by 3, then `M` scales that by 2, and the
composite `C = M·N` scales area by 6. The identity `det(AB) = det(A)·det(B)` —
which is a small chore to prove by grinding through the entries — is obvious
the instant you read the determinant as an area factor. Two scalings in a row
multiply. That's the whole proof.

## The payoff: when can you undo a matrix?

Now the question everything has been building toward. A transformation is
*invertible* when you can undo it — when every landing spot came from exactly
one starting point, so you can always find your way back.

Watch what a determinant of zero does to that. Here `det(M) = 0`, and the grid
has collapsed onto a single line. Every point on that line is the image of a
whole line's worth of starting points — they've been piled on top of each
other. The information about where things came from is *gone*. And the row
`inv(M)` says exactly that:

<WarpEmbed
  state={DET_INVERT}
  caption="det(M) = 0 flattens the plane onto a line — and inv(M) reports 'Not invertible (det = 0)'."
/>

There it is, the punchline the whole subject hinges on: **a matrix is
invertible exactly when its determinant is not zero.** Not as a rule to
memorize, but as something you just watched happen. Zero determinant means
space got flattened, flattening destroys information, and destroyed
information can't be recovered. One number — an area, a volume — told you
whether the transformation could be undone before you tried.
